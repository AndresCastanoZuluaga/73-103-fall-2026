## Topics

### Measuring GDP

Meetings 2 and 3, plus the accounting language from the GDP deck. Readings: CORE 3.1–3.4. Handout 1 is part of this topic and is not in the repo.

Formulas, as written on `slides/02_Measuring_GDP_F26_V2.pdf`:

- Expenditure: \(Y \equiv C + I + G + (X - M)\), with \(NX = X - M\).
- Value added: \(VA_i = P_i Q_i - \text{intermediates}_i\), and \(Y = \sum VA_i\).
- Income: \(Y = W + R + \text{profits} + \text{other factor payments}\).
- The three are equal when the accounts are complete. Inventories sit in \(I\), so unsold output is still counted.
- Consumption on the same deck, preview of the goods market: \(C = C_0 + c_1(Y - T)\), with \(C_0 \ge 0\) and \(0 < c_1 < 1\). New housing is \(I\), not \(C\). Imputed rent of an owner-occupied house is \(C\).
- Government: \(G = \bar{G}\). Transfers are not \(G\). The retiree’s spending of a transfer is already in \(C\).
- A share decomposition used on the deck: \(\Delta Y \approx s_C \Delta C + s_I \Delta I + s_G \Delta G + s_{NX} \Delta NX\).

Applications from that deck:

- United States shares on the slide: \(C\) 68%, \(I\) 18%, \(G\) 17%, \(NX\) −3%. Qatar: \(C\) 22%, \(I\) 38%, \(G\) 12%, \(NX\) +28%.
- QatarEnergy North Field modules, built in Korea for QR 800 and installed in Qatar for QR 50. Then \(\Delta C = 0\), \(\Delta I = 850\), \(\Delta G = 0\), \(\Delta NX = -800\), \(\Delta Y = 50\). Only installation value added is Qatar’s GDP.
- A Doha household’s imported phone, retail QR 4,000, import cost QR 3,500, retailer margin QR 500. Then \(\Delta C = 4000\), \(\Delta I = 0\), \(\Delta G = 0\), \(\Delta NX = -3500\), \(\Delta Y = 500\).
- In this year’s GDP or not, from the same deck: Lusail stadium built in 2019 (investment then, not this year); resale of a 2022 World Cup ticket (not current production); a new LNG train with Korean modules ( \(I\) and \(-M\), GDP only by Qatari value added); a 2019 Land Cruiser on Qatar Living (used good; a dealer’s margin would be current value added).
- Buying QNB shares or a Treasury bill is not \(I\).

Questions:

1. Repeat the phone and the modules with one change each. A tourist buys the phone. A Qatari yard builds the modules. Students should move the margin into exports in the first variant, and move the QR 800 from imports into domestic value added in the second.
2. Classify eight short items in one pass: a new school, unemployment insurance, inventory accumulation, a used car, a haircut, intermediate steel, a foreign factory’s output inside Qatar, a household’s purchase of newly issued equity. The school is \(G\). The insurance check is a transfer, and the spending of it is \(C\). Inventories are \(I\). The used car is out, apart from any dealer margin. The haircut is \(C\). The steel is out as an intermediate if it is already in the final good. The foreign factory’s Qatari output is in Qatar’s GDP. The equity purchase is a financial transaction.
3. Why can \(C + I + G + NX\) exceed 100% of GDP in one country’s published shares and still be an identity? Because \(NX\) can be negative. Ask them to check the US line: \(68 + 18 + 17 - 3 = 100\).
4. A firm produces more than it sells. Which component moves, and what happens to next period’s production if demand does not? Inventories, inside \(I\). That is the bridge to the goods-market adjustment on the ZZ diagram. Do not turn this into an accelerator model.

### Real GDP, PPP, the cycle, and the limits of GDP

Meeting 3. CORE 3.5. Blanchard chapter 10, pp. 201–203 only. The three Blanchard pages are the opening on the standard of living, output per person. They are not the production function and not growth accounting. Those are the November growth block.

Formulas on the GDP deck:

- Nominal GDP: \(\sum P_t Q_t\).
- Real GDP: \(\sum P_{\text{base}} Q_t\). In the base year the two are equal.
- GDP deflator \(= (\text{nominal GDP} / \text{real GDP}) \times 100\). It is 100 in the base year.
- Inflation from a price index: \(100 \times (\text{new} - \text{old}) / \text{old}\).
- PPP GDP \(= \text{nominal GDP in local currency} / \text{PPP conversion factor}\). The deck’s chicken example: 100 INR per kg and 5 dollars per kg gives 20 INR per international dollar, against a market rate of about 83.
- Years to double \(\approx 70 / \text{annual growth rate}\). At 2%, about 35 years. At 7%, about 10.
- Standard of living in the assigned Blanchard pages: output per person. The deck’s cross-country comparison is the PPP column, not the market-exchange-rate column.

Worked numbers already on the deck, so the packet should not contradict them:

- Baklava and umm ali, base year 2019. Nominal GDP: 7,000, then 10,000, then 13,050. Real GDP: 7,000, then 9,000, then 10,750. Deflator: 100, 111.1, 121.4. Inflation: 11.1%, then 9.3%.
- Cookies and smartphones, base 2019. Nominal GDP in 2019 is 167,400. Real GDP in 2020 is 181,000. The 2021 deflator is 134.4.
- Philippines and Qatar, as printed on the slide: total nominal GDP 404 billion dollars and 237 billion; GDP per capita 3,500 and 82,000 dollars; PPP per capita 10,000 and 115,000.

Applications:

- Five ways nominal GDP can rise: quantity up, price up, both up, price up by more than quantity falls, quantity up by more than price falls. Real GDP is how you separate them. The deck’s Qatar point: real GDP can rise in a year when nominal GDP falls.
- Rule of thumb on the slide: compare years with real GDP, compare countries with PPP.
- The cycle, as drawn in class: peak, contraction, trough, expansion. Two recession descriptions were both on the slide: a significant decline spread across the economy, illustrated with two consecutive quarters, and output below normal even if it is growing again. Examples named: United States 2001, 2008–09, 2020.
- Investment’s share can be modest and still dominate \(\Delta Y\), because it is volatile. The slide’s 2009 line: investment is about a fifth of US GDP and accounted for most of the fall.
- Three holes in the print, from the deck: quality change (the computer CPI versus the all-items CPI), unpaid household work, unregistered output. GDP is a production measure, not a well-being measure.

Questions:

1. From the cookie table, compute nominal GDP in 2021, real GDP in 2021, and the inflation rate of the deflator from 2020 to 2021. The deck already gives the 2021 deflator as 134.4 from nominal 255,750 and real 190,250. The new step is the inflation rate off a deflator they have to build for 2020 themselves.
2. Qatar’s nominal GDP falls and real GDP rises. What must have happened to the deflator? It fell. Ask whether a household’s imported consumption basket necessarily got cheaper. It need not have. That is the handoff to the CPI.
3. Whose living standard is the Philippines–Qatar table about: the country, or the person? Total GDP says the Philippines is larger. Per-person PPP says Qatar is richer. Blanchard’s three assigned pages are about the second question. Do not ask for a Solow residual.
4. Name one activity that raises well-being and not GDP, and one that raises GDP and need not raise well-being. Unpaid care is the first. Rebuilding after a disaster, or a shift from home cooking to restaurants, is the second. Keep it on the three limitations from the slide.

### The cost of living

Meeting 4, Wednesday 2 September. CORE 4.1–4.4. The lecture deck is `slides/03_Measuring_Prices.pdf`, which students cannot currently download. CORE 4.5–4.9 are not required. The unit note also says Mankiw chapters 10 and 11 are not required readings. The class still taught the CPI material below, so it is in the review. The formal Phillips curve is not.

Formulas and definitions from that deck:

- CPI \(= 100 \times (\text{cost of the fixed basket} / \text{cost of the same basket in the base year})\).
- Inflation \(= (\text{CPI}_t - \text{CPI}_{t-1}) / \text{CPI}_{t-1}\).
- Core CPI: the CPI without food and energy. PPI: prices paid by firms, treated in class as a leading indicator of the CPI. PCE and the trimmed-mean CPI were named as alternatives. Students should know what each one prices. They should not memorize a Truflation series.
- GDP deflator versus CPI. The deflator prices domestic production, including capital goods and exports, and it uses current quantities. The CPI prices the consumer basket, including imports, on fixed weights.
- Dollars of year \(T\) in today’s prices: \(\text{amount}_T \times (\text{CPI}_{\text{today}} / \text{CPI}_T)\).
- Real interest rate, as the deck stated it: nominal rate minus inflation. The exact Fisher equation was not on the slide. Do not add it.
- Quantity equation, as a cause of inflation on the same deck: \(MV = PQ\). If \(V\) and \(Q\) are relatively stable, a rise in \(M\) shows up in \(P\). The slide’s own caveats stay attached: unused capacity, and a liquidity trap. This is not a license to teach the money-market diagram inside the prices block. That diagram is the financial week.
- Causes named on the deck: cost-push, demand-pull, expectations, and the monetary channel above.

Applications and slide arithmetic:

- Baklava basket, 10 baklavas and 5 umm ali, base 2019. Basket costs 210, 240, 270. CPI 100, 114.3, 128.6.
- Lamb and beef, 10 lb and 20 lb, base 2017. CPI 100, 133.3, 200. Inflation from 2018 to 2019 is 50%.
- Tuition in 2018 dollars, CPI 130.7 in 1990 and 251.1 in 2018. Private 9,340 dollars becomes 17,944, a 99.7% real rise. Public four-year 1,908 becomes 3,665, a 179.1% real rise. Public two-year 906 becomes 1,741, a 110.3% real rise.
- Starbucks muffins: CPI and deflator both rise. Caterpillar tractors made in Illinois: deflator rises, CPI does not. Armani jeans imported to the United States: CPI rises, deflator does not.
- Three CPI biases, each in the direction of overstating the rise in the cost of living when quality and variety improve: substitution, new goods, unmeasured quality change. The iPhone 13 versus iPhone 14 at the same 799 dollar sticker price was the quality example.
- Indexation: a contract, a benefit, or a tax bracket that moves with a price index. Regional price differences were tied to where a firm puts a plant and to how a wage compares across cities.
- CORE 4.3 is the assigned “what is wrong with inflation” reading: purchasing power of a fixed nominal income, and redistribution between borrowers and lenders when inflation is not the inflation that was expected. CORE 4.4 is conflicting claims on output, still qualitative. The prices deck does not derive a Phillips curve, and this review does not either.

Questions:

1. Recompute the lamb CPI and the 2018–2019 inflation rate from the raw prices, without looking at the printed solution. Then change the basket to 20 lb of lamb and 10 lb of beef and say whether inflation rises or falls. The point is the weight, not a new index formula.
2. For each of muffins, tractors, and imported jeans, say what happens to the CPI and to the deflator, and why. Same three cases as the deck.
3. A nominal wage rises 4% and the CPI rises 6%. What happens to the real wage, and which index did you use? Real wage down about 2% on the deck’s subtraction. Use the CPI because the question is the cost of living, not the price of domestic capital goods.
4. Why can the CPI rise faster than the deflator in a year when imported energy is expensive? Imports are in the consumer basket and not in domestic production. The deck asked this about 2021. Do not require a 2021 data point that was not printed.
5. Name the three measurement problems and the direction of the bias. Then one sentence on why a fixed basket cannot see substitution.

Formulas:

- Real wage from CORE 1.4: nominal wage divided by the price level. The operational version students already practiced is the CPI conversion from meeting 4.
- Blanchard pp. 135–140, from the chapter contents, not from a local PDF: the stocks (\(E\), \(U\), the labor force) and the size of the flows (hires and separations). A high unemployment rate can coexist with a large number of hires if separations are also large. Page 141, wage determination, is the 28 October class. Do not review bargaining, efficiency wages, the natural rate, or wage-setting and price-setting.

### The goods market and the multiplier

Meetings 6 and 7. Blanchard chapter 3. Handout 2. The goods dashboard. CORE 3.6–3.8 are not required; do not rebuild the multiplier in CORE’s notation. There is no goods deck in the repo. The behavioral equations below are the ones the GDP deck already wrote down, closed up in the way Blanchard chapter 3 uses them. Where Handout 2 may have gone further, the draft has to wait for the handout.

Formulas:

- Demand for goods: \(Z = C + I + G\).
- \(C = C_0 + c_1(Y - T)\), \(0 < c_1 < 1\).
- In chapter 3, \(I = \bar{I}\) and \(G = \bar{G}\), and \(T\) is given. The GDP appendix also wrote \(I = I_0 - bi\) with \(b > 0\). That interest-rate form is the bridge to IS. Inside a pure goods-market problem, hold \(i\) fixed and treat investment as autonomous.
- Equilibrium: \(Y = Z\). Production adjusts to demand. If \(Y > Z\), inventories pile up and firms cut production. If \(Y < Z\), inventories fall and firms raise production.
- Solving: \(Y = \dfrac{1}{1 - c_1}\left(C_0 - c_1 T + \bar{I} + \bar{G}\right)\).
- Multiplier on autonomous spending: \(\dfrac{1}{1 - c_1}\). A rise in \(G\), in \(C_0\), or in \(\bar{I}\) is multiplied by that factor.
- Tax multiplier: \(\dfrac{\Delta Y}{\Delta T} = -\dfrac{c_1}{1 - c_1}\). A tax cut raises disposable income, and the first round is partly saved, so the tax multiplier is smaller in size than the spending multiplier.
- The geometric story: the first round of \(\Delta G\) is \(\Delta G\) itself; then \(c_1 \Delta G\); then \(c_1^2 \Delta G\); and the sum is \(\Delta G / (1 - c_1)\).
- Private saving \(S = Y - T - C = -C_0 + (1 - c_1)(Y - T)\). In the closed economy, equilibrium is \(I = S + (T - G)\).
- The open-economy shortcut on the GDP deck, \(NX = NX_0 - nY\), is labeled “later.” The open-economy unit is Blanchard 17–19 in November. Leave \(n\) out unless Handout 2 put it in. Same for a proportional tax \(T = tY\): include it only if Handout 2 or the 16 September class used it. The repo cannot confirm that.
- The real exchange rate definition on the GDP appendix, \(\varepsilon = EP / P^\*\), is also marked as Blanchard’s later convention. Do not examine it on Wednesday.

Applications:

- A fiscal impulse, which is the “use” cell on the course map for this topic.
- Qatar public spending as \(\bar{G}\), and North Field capex as the autonomous piece of \(I\). A change in animal spirits is a change in \(C_0\) or in \(\bar{I}\), not a movement along a curve they have not drawn yet.
- Quiz 2 was the goods-market quiz, on 16 September. The prompt is on Canvas, not here. The packet’s goods questions should look like that quiz in structure once the prompt is in hand: solve for \(Y\), then a shift.
- The dashboard is for slopes and shifts. It is not a source of exam parameters.

Questions:

1. \(C_0 = 200\), \(c_1 = 0.6\), \(T = 100\), \(\bar{I} = 150\), \(\bar{G} = 200\). Find equilibrium \(Y\), \(C\), and private saving. Then check \(I = S + (T - G)\). These numbers are a review construction. Replace them with Handout 2’s numbers if the handout uses a different set. Equilibrium \(Y = 2.5 \times (200 - 60 + 150 + 200) = 1{,}225\). \(C = 200 + 0.6(1{,}125) = 875\). Private saving \(= 1{,}225 - 100 - 875 = 250\). Public saving \(= 100 - 200 = -100\). Investment 150 equals 250 − 100.
2. From that equilibrium, raise \(G\) by 50. Then, instead, cut \(T\) by 50. Compare \(\Delta Y\). Spending: multiplier 2.5, \(\Delta Y = 125\). Tax cut: multiplier \(-0.6 / 0.4 = -1.5\) in the \(\Delta Y / \Delta T\) sense, so \(\Delta Y = 75\). This is the result Mago’s exercise is built to show.
3. Households try to save more, so \(C_0\) falls by 40 and \(c_1\) stays put. What happens to \(Y\), and what happens to equilibrium private saving? \(Y\) falls by \(2.5 \times 40 = 100\). Private saving need not rise one for one. Have them recompute \(S\). This is the paradox of saving. Do not call it a shift of IS unless you are in the last fifteen minutes and IS–LM 1 is still on the exam.
4. At the original equilibrium, demand comes in 80 above production. What do inventories do this period, and which way does \(Y\) move next? Inventories fall by 80. Firms raise production toward the equilibrium. Ask for the equilibrium, not for a multi-period path.

### The classroom simulation

Meeting 8, Monday 21 September. Mago (2014) and the Canvas support materials. The paper’s point, from its abstract: students often treat a dollar of government spending and a dollar of tax cuts as the same impulse. The exercise has the instructor act as the government and students act as firms and as worker-consumers, and it runs the successive rounds. It is also set up to discuss automatic stabilizers and why the classroom result is cleaner than a real stimulus (credit constraints, unemployment benefits, inflation, waste).

The round-by-round table that was actually used is not in this repo. Do not invent a six-student script and present it as Monday’s exercise. Replay the logic with the two multipliers from the previous topic, and label the rounds.

Formulas to put next to the story:

- Spending: rounds \(\Delta G,\ c_1\Delta G,\ c_1^2\Delta G,\ \ldots\), sum \(\Delta G/(1-c_1)\).
- Tax cut of the same size: the first round is only \(c_1 \Delta T\) in magnitude, because households save \(1 - c_1\). Sum \((c_1/(1-c_1))\Delta T\) in magnitude.
- If the class introduced a marginal tax rate as an automatic stabilizer, the multiplier shrinks. Include that line only after checking the Canvas packet.

Applications:

- The discussion due that day was the Mago discussion. A fair exam question is the comparison the paper exists to teach, not a recollection of who stood at the front of the room.
- Tie one round to Qatar: a public project (the first round is demand for local output) versus a transfer or a tax cut (the first round is partly saved). Keep transfers out of \(G\), which the GDP deck already insisted on.

Questions:

1. Why is the first round of a tax cut smaller than the first round of spending, before any multiplier arithmetic? Because the tax cut hits disposable income, and \(c_1 < 1\).
2. With \(c_1 = 0.6\), write the first three rounds of a 100 increase in \(G\) and the first three rounds of a 100 tax cut. Spending: 100, 60, 36. Tax cut: 60, 36, 21.6. The sums approach 250 and 150.
3. Name one reason the classroom multiplier overstates the effect of a real stimulus. Use the paper’s own list: some of the extra income cannot be spent because credit is tight, some leaks into imports or prices, benefits replace wages, or the project does not become demand one for one. One reason is enough. Do not grade a policy essay.

### Financial markets

Meetings 9–11. Blanchard chapter 4. Handout 3. The announcement includes this block. There is no financial deck and no Handout 3 in the repo. The formulas are the standard chapter 4 apparatus that the course map already named: \(M^d = \$Y\, L(i)\), \(i = \bar{i}\), open-market operations, banks and reserves, and the zero lower bound. Check every parameter and every reserve-ratio symbol against Handout 3 before the packet is copied. Blanchard chapter 6 and Mankiw chapter 16 are not required.

Formulas:

- A one-year discount bond with face value 100: \(P_B = 100 / (1 + i)\), so \(i = 100/P_B - 1\). Price and yield move in opposite directions.
- Money pays no interest. Bonds pay \(i\). Higher \(i\) raises the opportunity cost of holding money, so \(L(i)\) slopes down. Higher nominal income \($Y\) raises transactions demand.
- Money-market equilibrium: \(M^s = \$Y\, L(i)\). With \(M^s\) set by the central bank, this pins down \(i\). An open-market purchase of bonds raises \(M^s\) and lowers \(i\). A rise in \($Y\) shifts money demand right and raises \(i\), unless the central bank accommodates it.
- The course map also writes \(i = \bar{i}\): the central bank picks the policy rate, and the money supply is whatever that rate requires. Both pictures were on the map. In a chapter 4 problem, use the upward or vertical supply and the downward demand. Save the horizontal \(i = \bar{i}\) line for the IS–LM sheet, and tell students they are the same institution seen two ways.
- Banks. Central-bank money is currency plus reserves. If the public holds no currency and banks hold reserves equal to a fraction \(\theta\) of deposits, deposits \(= H / \theta\) and the money supply is that stock of deposits. A higher reserve ratio supports less money for a given \(H\).
- If the handout used a currency ratio \(c\) as well, the multiplier is \((1 + c) / (c + \theta)\). Use that version only if Handout 3 does. The repo does not contain the handout, so the packet’s default is the no-currency case, with one sentence that currency leakage makes the multiplier smaller.
- Zero lower bound, the 30 September topic. Near \(i = 0\), money and bonds are close substitutes, money demand is flat, and a further open-market purchase does not reduce \(i\). The prices deck had already named the liquidity trap as the caveat to \(MV = PQ\). This is the diagram that matches that caveat. Do not add quantitative easing, reserve remuneration, or a risk premium. Those are not in the assigned chapter-4 week as the notes describe it, and chapter 6 is explicitly not required.

Applications:

- The course-map use is “QCB / Fed; \(i = \bar{i}\)”: a policy rate, not a market that appears from nowhere.
- Meeting 1’s yen-carry narrative is the application students already know, and it does not need uncovered interest parity. Borrow yen at a low rate (the slide: 0.1%), convert at 162 yen per dollar, hold higher-yielding dollar assets. The Bank of Japan moves the policy rate to 0.25%. The yen appreciates (the slide: toward 146, about 12%). Levered positions unwind, margin calls follow, and the Nikkei and the S&P fall (the slide: about −17% and −8% over those days). The exam question, if any, is this chain in words. It is not a Blanchard 17 model.
- A higher policy rate is also the real-interest-rate idea from the prices deck, once inflation is subtracted. Keep the two rates labeled. The money market determines a nominal \(i\).

Questions:

1. A bond pays 100 in one year. Its price is 96. What is \(i\)? Then the central bank’s purchase pushes the price to 98. What happens to \(i\)? First, \(i = 100/96 - 1 = 4.167\%\). Then \(i = 100/98 - 1 = 2.041\%\). Price up, yield down.
2. Draw money demand and a vertical money supply. Show \($Y\) up, then an open-market sale. Demand shifts right and \(i\) rises. A sale cuts \(M^s\) and \(i\) rises again. Ask which of those two the central bank would do if it wanted to hold \(i\) fixed after the income increase. It would have to expand \(M^s\).
3. \(H = 500\) and \(\theta = 0.1\), no currency. How large are deposits? What if \(\theta\) rises to 0.25? Deposits 5,000, then 2,000. Ask why a reserve requirement is not the same object as a tax. It is not \(T\).
4. At \(i = 0\), the central bank buys bonds and \(H\) rises. What happens to \(i\) and to \(Y\) if investment depends on \(i\) and goods demand is otherwise unchanged? \(i\) stays at 0. \(I\) does not rise. \(Y\) does not rise through this channel. The spending multiplier is the tool that still works. That sentence is the farthest this review goes toward IS–LM.
5. In the yen-carry story, which price moved first, the policy rate or the exchange rate, and why did a leveraged trader have to sell dollar assets? The rate moved first. The yen appreciation shrank the dollar value of the yen debt’s repayment, and the margin call forced the sale. Do not ask for \(\varepsilon = EP/P^\*\).

### IS–LM 1

Meeting 12 as announced. Blanchard chapter 5, first class only. The later meetings are out: “LM and the joint diagram” on 19 October, fiscal versus monetary policy on 21 October, the policy mix and the financial crisis on 26 October. Blanchard chapters 6 and 9 are not required. There is no slide deck. This block was not taught. The sheet exists so the packet matches the announcement until the announcement is edited.

Use only objects the earlier meetings have already named.

Formulas:

- Goods equilibrium at a given interest rate is the IS relation. Take \(I = I_0 - bi\) from the GDP appendix, hold \(C_0\), \(T\), and \(G\) fixed, and the equilibrium condition from chapter 3 becomes \(Y = \dfrac{1}{1-c_1}(C_0 - c_1 T + I_0 - bi + G)\). Higher \(i\) means lower \(I\) and lower \(Y\). In \((Y, i)\) space the IS curve slopes down.
- Financial equilibrium, in the form the course map already printed: \(i = \bar{i}\). The alternative, also named on that map, is the chapter 4 money market \(M / \$Y = L(i)\), which slopes up if written as an LM curve in the same space. The first-class version to review is the horizontal line. Say once that the upward LM is the same market when the central bank sets \(M\) instead of \(\bar{i}\).
- Equilibrium is the pair \((Y, \bar{i})\) on IS.
- Fiscal expansion: \(G\) up or \(T\) down shifts IS right. Under \(i = \bar{i}\), \(Y\) rises by the goods-market multiplier and \(i\) does not change.
- Monetary expansion: the central bank cuts \(\bar{i}\). The economy moves down along IS. \(I\) rises. \(Y\) rises.
- Do not add a risk premium, expected inflation inside LM, a peg, or the crisis diagram.

Questions, three is enough:

1. Which schedule is the goods market, and which is the policy rate? IS is goods. The horizontal line is \(\bar{i}\).
2. \(G\) rises and the central bank keeps \(\bar{i}\). What happens to \(Y\), \(I\), and \(i\)? \(Y\) rises, \(i\) is unchanged, \(I\) is unchanged if it depends only on \(i\). If someone argues \(I\) rises because \(Y\) rose, they have added an accelerator the course has not used. Accept only \(I(i)\) unless Handout 2 already put \(I(Y)\).
3. The central bank cuts \(\bar{i}\) and \(G\) is fixed. What happens to \(I\) and \(Y\)? Both rise. Ask them to point at the movement along IS rather than a shift.

If the opening announcement is amended to drop IS–LM 1, delete this page from the packet and do not spend the last fifteen minutes on it.
